Queerology · Power

Power

A curated wall of concepts exploring how power regulates bodies, defines norms, classifies lives, produces knowledge, and decides who is protected, corrected, exposed, or abandoned.

Mechanism

Institutions

Power does not float above society as an abstract force. It lives in institutions: the family, school, clinic, prison, church, border regime, military, media, and state. Institutions make power durable by turning norms into routines. They decide what counts as healthy, legal, respectable, intelligent, deviant, risky, or worthy of protection.

Michel Foucault
Theorist of institutions, discipline, and modern power
Power becomes ordinary when it is built into the places that organize daily life.
Reading shelf
  • Discipline and Punish
  • The history of institutions and control
Norms

Normativity

Normativity is the quiet architecture of power. It defines what feels natural, proper, healthy, civilized, feminine, masculine, productive, or moral. Once a norm is established, difference can be framed as failure, pathology, danger, or defect. This is how power hides: not only by punishing deviation, but by making the norm feel like common sense.

Sara Ahmed
Scholar of norms, orientation, and institutional power
A norm is most effective when it disappears into the background and starts to feel like reality itself.
Reading shelf
  • Queer Phenomenology
  • Living a Feminist Life
Core concept

Biopolitics

Biopolitics describes how modern power manages life. It tracks populations, regulates reproduction, health, sexuality, hygiene, productivity, and risk. Instead of ruling only through spectacular punishment, modern states increasingly govern by optimizing life: measuring it, improving it, correcting it, and sorting it into normal and abnormal forms.

Michel Foucault
Philosopher of biopower, discipline, and state rationality
Modern power does not only threaten death. It administers life.
Reading shelf
  • The History of Sexuality, Vol. 1
  • Biopolitics overview
Afterlife of power

Necropolitics

Necropolitics asks a darker question: if biopolitics manages life, who gets pushed toward death, disposability, exposure, and abandonment? Some groups are not protected by the state but rendered killable, neglectable, or permanently vulnerable. Power does not only produce life chances. It also decides whose suffering is acceptable, invisible, or politically useful.

Achille Mbembe
Political theorist of sovereignty, violence, and death-worlds
Sovereignty is revealed most clearly in the power to decide who may live and who may be left to die.
Reading shelf
  • Necropolitics
  • Sovereignty, violence, and abandonment
Medical power

Medicalization

Medicalization is the process by which social, moral, sexual, or behavioral differences are redefined as medical issues. It gives institutions the authority to diagnose, classify, correct, and normalize. Queer lives, disabled bodies, reproductive choices, and nonconforming genders have often been governed through this lens. Medical language can sound neutral, even caring, while quietly converting difference into disorder and political control into treatment.

Peter Conrad
Sociologist of medicalization and diagnosis
To medicalize is often to move a question from ethics or politics into the authority of diagnosis.
Reading shelf
  • The Medicalization of Society
  • Pathologization and diagnosis
Violent optimization

Eugenics

Eugenics is the fantasy of improving the population by controlling who may reproduce, belong, or be considered fully valuable. It combines science, nationalism, ableism, racism, class hierarchy, and reproductive control. Eugenics is not just a historical horror. Its logic survives in sterilization, border sorting, genetic panic, fitness narratives, and policies that rank some lives as more desirable than others.

Angela Davis
Scholar of prisons, race, and reproductive justice
The dream of “better populations” is never neutral. It is built from hierarchies of whose future matters.
Reading shelf
  • Women, Race & Class
  • Histories of eugenics and reproductive control
Why it matters

Surveillance, Law, and Resistance

Power is not only top-down force. It is also the network of files, forms, data, police, diagnoses, documentation, school rules, border checks, algorithms, and public narratives that decide who becomes legible and who becomes suspect. But power is never total. People resist through refusal, community, mutual care, reclaiming language, breaking categories, exposing institutions, and building other ways to live. Power draws the map. Resistance redraws it.

Judith Butler
Philosopher of precarity, recognizability, and political resistance
Where life is constrained by power, politics begins with the struggle over whose lives become livable.
Reading shelf

Workplace power

Labor & Economic Power

Workplace authority is material before it becomes cultural. Employers and managers distribute pay, hours, assignments, information, visibility, references, contracts, and promotion. This direct control can be extended into indirect demands about attitude, sociability, belonging, and emotional display—making access to resources conditional on the performance of the “right” kind of worker.

  1. 01 Material dependence Income, time, access, security
  2. 02 Behavioral discipline Rules, metrics, evaluation, visibility
  3. 03 Emotional regulation Attitude, positivity, gratitude, calm
  4. 04 The managed self Identity, belonging, authenticity
Source of leverage

Resource Power

Employers do not need to issue explicit threats when they control resources workers need. Pay, hours, insurance, contracts, schedules, references, information, and future opportunities can be allocated, delayed, restricted, or withheld. The lever is direct even when the reason for using it remains unstated.

Key thinkers
  • Richard M. Emerson Power-dependence relations
  • Karl Marx Labor-power and wage dependence
  • Pierre Bourdieu Economic, social, and cultural capital
  • Kathi Weeks Feminist critique of work and the work ethic
Allocation and access

Organizational Gatekeeping

Workplace power operates through decisions about access: who is invited into meetings, given information, trained, mentored, assigned visible work, evaluated as promotable, or treated as a cultural fit. Formally neutral procedures can convert familiarity, similarity, and comfort into unequal careers.

Theory and research
  • Joan Acker Gendered organizations and the “ideal worker”
  • Lauren Rivera Hiring as cultural matching
  • Emilio Castilla & Stephen Benard The paradox of meritocracy
  • Kristen Schilt Trans workers and gendered workplace inequality
Control of conduct

Labor Discipline

Industrial discipline organized bodies through clocks, supervision, and repetitive routines. Contemporary organizations add targets, dashboards, visibility, team norms, and self-surveillance. Control may be imposed by management, embedded in systems, or enforced horizontally by coworkers.

Key thinkers
  • E. P. Thompson Time and work-discipline
  • Michael Burawoy Manufacturing consent
  • James R. Barker Concertive and peer-enforced control
  • Michel Foucault Discipline, observation, and normalization
Compulsory feeling

Emotional Labor

Work may require more than performing a task. Workers can also be expected to display, cultivate, or suppress particular feelings: warmth, calm, optimism, patience, gratitude, or enthusiasm. Emotional labor becomes coercive when refusing the required performance threatens evaluation, resources, safety, or belonging.

Theory and research
  • Arlie Russell Hochschild Feeling rules and the managed heart
  • Alicia A. Grandey Surface acting, deep acting, and strain
  • Adia Harvey Wingfield Racialized feeling rules
  • Amy J. Tweedy Queering emotional labor
Emotional hierarchy

Affective Power

Affective power determines whose emotions are treated as legitimate. Some workers may express authority, anger, exhaustion, or dissatisfaction; others must reassure, absorb tension, and remain pleasant. Complaint can be recoded as negativity, while compulsory optimism is presented as professionalism.

Key thinkers
  • Sara Ahmed Happiness duties and institutional complaint
  • Lauren Berlant Cruel optimism and affective attachment
  • Melissa Gregg Work, intimacy, and emotional availability
  • Diefendorff et al. Power and workplace display rules
Optional, but not really

Compulsory Participation

Extra-role work, team bonding, workplace fun, book clubs, personal reflections, social events, and displays of enthusiasm may be described as voluntary. Yet nonparticipation can be interpreted as poor attitude, weak commitment, or failure to be a team player. The activity is formally optional while the social and professional consequences are not.

Theory and research
  • Eran Vigoda-Gadot Compulsory citizenship behavior
  • Mark Bolino et al. Citizenship behavior under pressure
  • Peter Fleming Managed fun and organizational cynicism
  • Ethan Mollick & Nancy Rothbard Mandatory fun, consent, and agency
Control of subjectivity

The Managed Self

Organizational culture can seek control not only over conduct but over commitment, personality, values, and self-understanding. Workers are encouraged to monitor and reshape their inner selves until organizational demands appear as personal goals, choices, and attitudes.

Key thinkers
  • Gideon Kunda Corporate culture and normative control
  • Hugh Willmott Culture as regulation of subjectivity
  • Nikolas Rose Governing through the psychological self
  • Catherine Casey Workplace community and identity integration
From privacy to performed identity

Coerced Authenticity & Compulsory Belonging

The older workplace command was to leave personal life at home. The newer organization may invite workers to “bring your whole self”—while deciding which parts of that self are relatable, useful, safe, or promotable. Inclusion can become another form of control when disclosure, vulnerability, sociability, or visible belonging is treated as a professional obligation.

Theory and queer workplace research
  • Peter Fleming & Andrew Sturdy “Just be yourself” and neo-normative control
  • Kenji Yoshino Covering and demands to minimize difference
  • Belle Rose Ragins Disclosure, fear, and workplace climate
  • Martinez, Webster, Mohr et al. Queer and trans authenticity in supportive contexts

Supporting a person’s freedom to be authentic is not the same as requiring disclosure, vulnerability, visibility, or emotional access.

The power is  material. The requirement is  emotional. The justification is  cultural.
